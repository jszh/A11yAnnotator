# Annotation reliability (IRR) and corpus hygiene — `eval/act-augmented/`

**Run 2026-08-14, repo at `a349d5b0`.** Inputs: the three human annotation exports in `annotations/`
(Mengqi, Davin, Ajit — 213 cases each, 426 unique pages, 10 SCs), the 926-page corpus, and
`_annotator/manifest.json`. All figures below are regenerated by the tools listed at the end; the
backing JSON lives in `eval/act-augmented/_annotator/irr/`.

Three things came out of this pass, in descending order of consequence for the paper:

1. **The corpus was leaking its own answer key.** 861 of 926 served pages carried an authoring
   comment that stated the verdict outright (`SC 1.1.1 FAILURE (F30 — text alternative that is not
   an alternative)`). Those are now stripped and the strip is verified byte-for-byte safe. A
   **second** leak class survives: 119 pages *render* the explanation as page copy, and 6 pages
   carry a machine-readable `data-intended-classification` attribute. This is a benchmark-validity
   issue, not a cosmetic one.
2. **Reliability is moderate-to-substantial, and it is anchored, not blind.** Pooled Cohen's
   κ = 0.628 (95% CI 0.505–0.751), Krippendorff's α = 0.629. But the annotation UI shows each coder
   the *claimed failure mechanism before* they answer Q1 — so this measures agreement given the
   answer's description, which is an upper bound on blind agreement, not an estimate of it.
3. **The corpus's ground-truth labels are not settled.** Of the flagged cases adjudicated one by one
   against the live pages, a substantial minority are confirmed defective — several ship a page that
   is not the page their metadata describes.

---

## 1. Study design (what the IRR can and cannot say)

The work split makes this **two independent pairs**, not a three-way panel:

| annotator | split file | n | overlaps with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mengqi | `annotator-1-first-half.json` | 213 | Ajit (107) |
| Davin | `annotator-2-second-half.json` | 213 | Ajit (106) |
| Ajit | `annotator-3-q1-q4.json` | 213 | both halves |

426 unique pages: **213 double-coded, 213 single-coded**. There is no unit with three coders, so
Fleiss' κ is not applicable; per-pair Cohen's κ is the primary statistic and Krippendorff's α is
reported because it handles the missing-data structure natively.

**Q1** (`issueExists`, yes/no — *"does an accessibility issue exist on this page?"*) is the
reliability-bearing judgment. **Q2** (`disposition`, approve/comment) is a workflow action and is
reported separately — it is not a second opinion about the page.

### The anchoring caveat

`_annotator/index.html:1303-1304` renders the per-page **`mechanism`** — the corpus's own prose
description of the intended failure — into page 1 of the wizard, and Q1 is answered on page 1
(`index.html:1344-1348`). `whyAutomatedToolsMiss` is held back to page 2, after Q1. So coders were
told what the planted defect was before judging whether it exists. That is defensible for a
*validation* task ("is this described defect really here?") but it means **κ = 0.63 is not a blind
inter-rater agreement figure** and should never be reported as one. Any paper claim should read
"agreement on confirming a described defect", and note that even so, coders diverged on 14% of pages.

## 2. Q1 inter-rater reliability

| pair | n | P₀ | κ (95% CI) | Gwet AC₁ | PABAK | prevalence idx | bias idx | McNemar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Davin × Ajit | 106 | 89.6% | **0.743** (0.599–0.887) — substantial | 0.827 | 0.792 | 0.443 | 0.066 | χ²=3.27, p=.070 → symmetric |
| Mengqi × Ajit | 107 | 82.2% | **0.498** (0.293–0.703) — moderate | 0.726 | 0.645 | 0.542 | −0.028 | χ²=0.21, p=.646 → symmetric |
| **pooled (both pairs stacked)** | 213 | 85.9% | **0.628** (0.505–0.751) | 0.773 | 0.718 | — | — | — |
| Krippendorff α (nominal, all 426 units) | 426 | — | **0.629** | — | — | — | — | — |

Confusion, Davin|Ajit: `yes|yes` 71, `no|no` 24, `yes|no` 9, `no|yes` 2.
Confusion, Mengqi|Ajit: `yes|yes` 73, `no|no` 15, `yes|no` 8, `no|yes` 11.

**Read κ against AC₁ here.** "Issue exists" is true on ~78% of pages, and that skew is what pushes κ
(0.628) well below raw agreement (85.9%) and below AC₁ (0.773) — the prevalence index is 0.44–0.54.
The honest summary is *substantial agreement on a heavily skewed binary*, and reporting κ alone
understates it while reporting P₀ alone overstates it.

**Neither pair shows a calibration offset.** McNemar is non-significant in both (p = .070 and .646),
so disagreement is symmetric noise rather than one coder being systematically stricter. That matters
for remediation: a tie-break rule or a leniency adjustment would not help; the disagreements are
genuinely hard or genuinely ambiguous cases.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

1.3.1 is worse than chance, but n = 11 — that is a signal to double-code more 1.3.1, not a finding.
The reliable read is the ordering: **2.4.2 (a mechanical check) is perfect; 1.4.1 and 1.3.1 (semantic
judgments) are the least reliable** — which is exactly the gradient you would predict, and is worth
stating in the paper as evidence that the corpus's hard cases are hard for humans too.

### Q2 disposition is not a second judgment

| pair | n | P₀ | κ | comment rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Davin × Ajit | 106 | 87.7% | 0.075 | 4.7% vs 9.4% |
| Mengqi × Ajit | 107 | 72.9% | 0.032 | 25.2% vs 5.6% |

κ ≈ 0 with high P₀ is the classic signature of a rare event coded to different personal thresholds:
Mengqi commented on 25% of pages, Ajit on 6%. **Do not report Q2 as an IRR figure** — it measures
annotator verbosity, not agreement about pages.

### Agreement with the corpus's own label

| annotator | n | agrees with `expected` | κ | says "no issue" where corpus says issue | says "issue" where corpus says clean |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Davin | 213 | 96.7% | 0.909 | 1 | 6 |
| Ajit | 213 | 95.3% | 0.876 | 7 | 3 |
| Mengqi | 213 | 81.7% | 0.382 | 17 | 22 |

Mengqi is the outlier by a wide margin, and the disagreement is two-sided (17 vs 22), i.e. noise
rather than a stricter standard. Adjudication bears this out: of Mengqi's bare label contradictions
that reached case-by-case review, the large majority were **refuted against the live page**. Practical
consequence: **a bare Mengqi contradiction with no comment text is weak evidence** and should not on
its own move a ground-truth label.

## 3. The answer-key leak

### 3a. Comments — found, stripped, verified

`_tools/strip-case-comments.js` removed **1978 HTML + 1236 JS + 1192 CSS comments** across 925 of 926
pages. 847 of the HTML comments contained explicit verdict language. Human annotations prove the leak
was live: several read *"…as mentioned in the comment"*, e.g. Davin on 2.1.2 —
*"The actual documented keystroke is Ctrl+W and not Ctrl+Q as mentioned in the comments"*.

Safety was not left to a regex. Comments were located with a real tokenizer per language (a
spec-shaped HTML scanner that tracks raw-text elements and quoted attributes; `esprima.tokenize` for
JS; a string/`url()`-aware scanner for CSS). Removal rules preserve semantics: JS block comments are
replaced by a newline when they spanned one (so ASI is unchanged); CSS comments are removed only when
whitespace already sits on a side (0 cases needed the unsafe path); a comment alone on its line takes
its line, except inside `<pre>`, where that whitespace is rendered content (5 pages).

**Verification — `_tools/verify-comment-strip.js`, all 925 pages, four independent checks:**

| check | method | result |
|---|---|---|
| checksum | sha256 of every original + stripped file vs the backup manifest | **0 mismatches** |
| DOM | browser `DOMParser` on both sources, scripts *not* executing, serialised without comment nodes; text exact inside `<pre>`/`<textarea>`, whitespace-collapsed elsewhere | **0 mismatches** |
| JS | `esprima` token stream (type+value, comments excluded) per inline script | **0 mismatches** |
| CSS | original with comments removed by the same scanner vs stripped, whitespace-normalised | **0 mismatches** |
| render | full-page screenshots, scripts running, byte-compared | **0 real mismatches** (2 apparent diffs were contention flake — identical over 6 repetitions each; 6 more were the harness rendering the backup copy where sibling PNGs 404) |

Backup: `eval/act-augmented/_archive/comment-strip-backups/2026-08-14T08-39-15/` (every original file +
sha256 manifest + `removed-comments.json` keeping the authored rationale queryable). One command
restores: `node eval/act-augmented/_tools/strip-case-comments.js --restore <dir>`.

### 3b. Leaks that survive — this is the open item

Stripping comments removed the leak that lives in *source*. The adjudicators found the leak also
lives in the *served DOM*, where removing it is a content change and therefore a decision, not a
cleanup. `_tools/scan-answer-leaks.js` quantifies it:

| class | files | example |
|---|---|---|
| **attribute** — machine-readable answer key | **6** | `4.1.3/is-it-a-status-message-scope-boundary/case-0*.html`: `<body data-intended-classification="inapplicable">`. Nothing in the repo reads it. |
| **prose** — the explanation rendered as page copy | **119** | `1.1.1/complex-image-long-description-incomplete/case-01.html`: *"Why automated checkers pass this page: the svg role="img" has a non-empty accessible name…"* |
| weak signal only (page merely says "WCAG"/"F13") | 4 | plausibly legitimate page content |

Prose leaks by SC: 4.1.3 (29), 1.3.2 (17), 2.1.2 (15), 1.4.11 (10), 2.4.7 (9), 1.1.1 (6), 3.3.1 (6),
2.1.1 (5), 2.4.6 (5), 3.3.2 (5), 4.1.2 (5), 1.4.13 (4), 2.4.4 (2), 1.4.3 (1).

**Did the leak contaminate the human labels?** Tested directly (`_tools/irr-leak-contrast.js`), and
the answer is **no detectable effect**:

| stratum | annotations | agreement with corpus label | double-coded n | P₀ | κ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| page still leaks | 83 | 92.8% | 21 | 76.2% | 0.314 |
| page clean | 556 | 91.0% | 192 | 87.0% | 0.660 |

Difference 1.8 pp, z = 0.53 — not significant. If anything agreement is *lower* on leaked pages. So
the human annotation round is not invalidated. **The exposure is forward-looking**: any LLM lane or
checker prompt that reads rendered text still gets told the answer on 119 pages, and that would
inflate measured performance. Recommendation: strip the 6 attributes (zero rendering impact,
zero readers) and make a deliberate call on the 119 prose blocks before the corpus is used as a
scored benchmark.

## 4. Which test cases are unreliable

`_tools/flag-unreliable-cases.js` ranks the 426 annotated cases by the signals in the annotations.
A `comment` disposition is **not** automatically a complaint: when a coder's own yes/no matches the
corpus label and they left no text, the comment is concordant bookkeeping. Separating those:

| signal | cases | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| CONTRADICTED | 24 | every coder who saw it disagrees with `expected` — not annotator noise |
| SPLIT | 30 | two coders saw it and disagreed with each other |
| FLAGGED | 70 | a coder actually reported a defect in the case |
| CONCORDANT-COMMENT | 17 | commented while agreeing — excluded from the complaint set |

**109 of 426 cases (25.6%) carry at least one signal; 83 are genuine complaints.**
Full inventory with per-coder detail: `_annotator/irr/unreliable-cases.json`.

## 5. Adjudication

100 cases (the complaint set plus the 17 concordant comments, batched before the refinement) were
adjudicated by 10 parallel agents working case-by-case against the live pages — reading the stripped
page, the pre-strip authored comments, and the metadata, and **probing dynamic claims** with
`_tools/probe-case.js` (tab-order walk, hover/Esc persistence, live-region announcement capture,
screenshots) rather than reasoning about them. Adjudicators could not edit files; they proposed
patches with exact anchors, which `_tools/apply-adjudication.js` validates for uniqueness before
applying.

One rule was imposed up front: **flipping a ground-truth label is never an "obvious fix."** A label
change is a claim about WCAG conformance, and one annotator's opinion is not sufficient warrant —
those cases are tagged `groundTruthDisputed` for human resolution instead.

### Outcome

| verdict | cases | |
|---|---|---|
| NO-DEFECT | 72 | complaint checked and refuted, or a judgment disagreement the rationale already answers |
| NEEDS-VALIDATION | 18 | plausible, but resolving it is a ground-truth call |
| FIX-OBVIOUS | 10 | verified defect with a mechanical repair |

Of the 10 FIX-OBVIOUS, **7 were applied** and **3 were held after I reviewed the patch itself**:

- **Applied (7)** — all metadata-only edits to `<sc>/result.json` re-syncing `scenario` / `mechanism` /
  `primarySelector` / `whyAutomatedToolsMiss` / `citation` to the page that actually ships. **No
  `expected` label changed** (verified: 0 `"expected"` lines in the diff), all four touched
  `result.json` files re-parse, and `manifest.json` was regenerated (26 lines, confined to those
  fields). Backup + patch log: `_archive/adjudication-fixes/2026-08-14T09-03-57/`.
- **Held (3)** — two were single-page instances of the 119-page prose-leak class, where patching one
  page leaves the corpus inconsistent; they belong to the systemic decision in §3b. The third is
  more interesting and is described below.

### The held page fix — why "obvious defect" ≠ "obvious repair"

`1.4.13::persistent-auto-timeout-vs-valid-info-invalidation::case-01`. Davin wrote *"When hovering or
clicking on the questionmark/info icon (the tool tip), nothing pops up for the user"* — and he is
right. The tooltip is `position:absolute` with `left:50%; top:130%`, written for a positioned parent
it does not have (`#limitTip` is a sibling of the trigger's label, and the only `position:relative`
is on the button itself), so it renders at **(500, 1170)** while its trigger sits at **(331, 221)** —
roughly 950px away, off-page. The adjudicator's repair is correct on that axis: probing the patched
copy puts the tooltip at (203, 260), directly under the trigger, and the *documented* defect (auto-
dismiss at 4s while still hovered) survives, so `expected: failed` would stand.

I checked the patch myself before applying it, and it has a side effect the adjudicator did not
report: it lifts the trigger `<button>` out of `<label for="amt">`, which changes the input's
accessible name from **"Amount (USD) About the daily transfer limit"** to **"Amount (USD)"**
(measured via CDP `Accessibility.getPartialAXTree`, before and after). Changing an accessible name in
a WCAG fixture is a substantive change to what the page tests, and the tooltip's CSS admits no
narrower repair. So: defect confirmed, repair held for the corpus owner, with both measurements
recorded in the tag. This is the line the "never auto-flip ground truth" rule is protecting.

### What the open cases look like

**21 cases are tagged `needs-validation`** (18 adjudicated as such + 3 held), listed highest-priority
first in `_annotator/irr/needs-validation.md`. Of those, **19 are `groundTruthDisputed`** and **7 are
`internallyContradictory`** — the case's own authored text contradicts its own label.

The dominant failure mode is not a wrong judgment call, it is **metadata drift: the shipped page is
not the page the metadata describes.** Examples the adjudicators verified against live pages:

- `2.1.2::documented-exit…::case-05` — metadata describes an LMS grader documenting `Ctrl+Q`,
  labelled `passed`; the shipped page is a translation booth documenting `Ctrl+W`, and a keyboard
  drive confirms a **real trap** (focus never leaves `select#src` over 8 Tabs). The case's own `.md`
  says FAIL. Davin's complaint ("the actual keystroke is Ctrl+W not Ctrl+Q") was right and understated.
- `2.4.4::duplicate-name-same-context…::case-02` and `case-06` — both carry `passed` metadata for
  pages that were never shipped (a charity download page, a volunteer conference); the actual pages
  are a swim school and a genetics course, and both `.md` files say FAIL. These are the aspect's
  **only two pass controls**, so the aspect currently has no valid negative control.
- `2.1.2::input-gate-required-interaction-exception::case-06` — metadata's `primarySelector` `#thumb`
  matches nothing; the page is an EHR modal with no trap (Alt+X exits and restores focus).

Distribution of what was actually wrong, across all 100 adjudicated: `metadata-wrong` 12,
`page-implementation` 7, `label-wrong` 7, `aspect-mismatch` 2, none 72.

### What this says about the annotators

Adjudicated per complaint: **39 confirmed, 37 refuted, 24 indeterminate**; complaint clarity **47
clear, 30 vague, 23 unintelligible**. So roughly half of the flags were actionable and half were
noise — which is consistent with the κ spread, and is why every flag was checked against a live page
rather than taken at face value. Two systematic annotator behaviours are worth recording:

- Bare label contradictions with no comment text (predominantly Mengqi's) were **usually refuted**.
- Several of Ajit's "false positive" comments land on **pass controls**, where he was reacting to the
  failure-worded *aspect title* displayed above a deliberate pass page. That is a UI presentation
  problem, not a page defect — the annotator was misled by the tool, and the fix is presentational.
