A Letter Home — Pvt. Edwin Marsden to his mother, June 1944

Somewhere in England
the 9th of June

Dear Mother,

I am writing this on a borrowed table with a stub of pencil, so forgive the hand. We landed two weeks ago after a crossing I would not wish on anyone — six days of grey water and a sea that never once sat still. Half the company was green to the gills and I will admit I was no hero among them. But we are ashore now, dry, and that is the main thing, so do not let yourself worry on that account.

The weather here has been a queer business. One hour it is soft rain that gets into everything, and the next the sun comes through and the whole country goes the most ridiculous green you ever saw. The hedges are taller than a man and the lanes wind about so that I have given up trying to know which way is home. A farmer's wife near our billet sells eggs and brown bread, and last Tuesday she gave me a jar of damson jam for nothing at all when she heard I had a mother who keeps bees. I told her about your hives and she would not take a penny.

Which brings me to the money. There should be a portion of my pay coming through to you by the end of the month, and I want you to put none of it by for me — spend it on the roof before the autumn, and get Mrs. Pell in to look at it properly this time instead of letting Tom climb up there with a bucket of tar. You know how that ended last year.

Speaking of Tom — has he had his medical yet? Tell him from me that if he is set on joining he is to finish his apprenticeship first, every last week of it, and not throw it over the way he throws over everything the moment it stops being new. A trade keeps a man fed when nothing else will. He will say I am preaching. Let him. I would give a good deal to be back at a quiet bench with a job I understood from start to finish.

The food, since I know you will ask, is plain but there is enough of it, and twice now we have had real coffee. I have grown almost fond of the tinned stew, which tells you something about how far a body can be brought down. When this is over the first thing I want is your Sunday roast and the potatoes done in the dripping, and I want to eat it slowly at the kitchen table with the window open.

I cannot tell you where we are going or when, and you would not want me to even if I could. So I will only say that I am well, that I am with good men, and that I think of the garden and the kitchen and the bees a great deal more than is probably good for a soldier. Give my love to Aunt Rose, kiss the dog for me though he will not thank you for it, and write soon — a letter from home is worth more than anyone at home can guess.

Your loving son,

Edwin

P.S. — If the damsons come good this year, save me a jar. I have made a promise to a farmer's wife to compare.
