The Marsh Otter Returns to the Lower Wye After Forty Quiet Years

By Helena Coombe, Estuary Correspondent

For four decades the saltmarsh below Brockweir held no otters. The reed beds were there, the eel runs were there, but the animals that once threaded the creeks at dusk had gone, pushed out by run-off from the upstream tanneries and the slow silting of the side channels. This spring, a volunteer with a thermal scope counted three.

The recovery did not happen on its own. Beginning in 2016 the Wye Estuary Trust re-dug six of the silted side channels by hand, restoring the brackish gradient the otters' prey depend on. They fenced the cattle back from the bank. They persuaded the last working tannery to switch to a closed-loop tanning bath. None of it was dramatic; all of it took years.

"You don't bring otters back," said the Trust's field officer. "You make a place where otters decide to come back. Then you wait, and you keep your mouth shut about exactly where."

That last part is deliberate. The Trust has declined to publish the precise location of the holt, citing the risk of disturbance from well-meaning visitors. Instead it is running guided dusk walks twice a month, capped at eight people, booked through the Brockweir village hall. The first three walks sold out within a day.

Ecologists caution against reading too much into three animals. An otter territory on a productive estuary can span ten kilometres of bank; three sightings might mean three otters, or one otter seen three times. A formal survey using spraint analysis and camera traps is planned for the autumn, once the eel run peaks and the otters are most active along the lower creeks.

Still, for the volunteers who spent eight winters in waders pulling silt out of the side channels, the count was its own reward. "We always said we were doing it for the otters," one of them said, "even when there weren't any otters. It felt slightly mad. It doesn't feel mad now."

The Trust's longer ambition is to link the Lower Wye marsh to the restored reaches upstream, so that a single continuous corridor of clean, fish-rich water runs from the tideway to the old mill weirs. That work depends on a Defra grant decision expected in October, and on the cooperation of nine separate riparian landowners, not all of whom are persuaded.

For now, the recommendation to anyone hoping to glimpse the returning otters is the same one the Trust gives on every walk: arrive before the light goes, stand still, watch the seam where the reeds meet the water, and be patient. The estuary, it turns out, rewards exactly the kind of slow attention that brought the otters back.
