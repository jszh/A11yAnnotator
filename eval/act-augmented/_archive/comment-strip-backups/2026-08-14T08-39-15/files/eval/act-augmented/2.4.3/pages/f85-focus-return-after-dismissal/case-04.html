The quiet grid: how a county utility learned to share its sunlight

By Naomi Treadwell · Climate · 9 min read

For three decades the substation on Quarry Road did one thing: it took power from the regional line and pushed it out to the eleven thousand homes of Harlan County. This spring, for the first time, it began to run in reverse.

The change is the result of a community-solar program that lets residents who cannot install their own panels — renters, apartment dwellers, anyone shaded by a north-facing roof — buy a share of a field of panels two miles away. The credits show up on their bill.

"We stopped thinking of the wire as a one-way street," the utility's engineer said. "Once you accept that power can flow both directions, a lot of old assumptions fall away."

The technical work was less about new hardware than about new permission. Reverse power flow had always been physically possible; what changed was the metering and the interconnection agreement that made it legal and legible to the billing system.

Other counties are watching. The state's rural cooperative association has scheduled four site visits this summer, and a neighboring district has already filed paperwork to copy the model.
