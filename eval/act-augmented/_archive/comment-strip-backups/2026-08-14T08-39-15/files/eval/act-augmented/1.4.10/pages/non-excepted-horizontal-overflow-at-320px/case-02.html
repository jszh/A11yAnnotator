Week 2 — Life in the Intertidal Zone

By the end of this lesson you will be able to: describe the physical stressors organisms face between the tides, and explain two adaptations that allow barnacles and mussels to survive desiccation.

The intertidal zone is one of the most physically demanding habitats on Earth. Twice a day it is submerged by the rising tide and twice a day it is exposed to air, sun, wind, and predators. An organism that lives here must tolerate being underwater and being bone-dry within the span of a few hours, and it must do so day after day for its entire life.

Zonation is the most visible result of these pressures. If you walk down a rocky shore from the high-water mark toward the sea, you pass through distinct bands of life. Near the top, lichens and periwinkles endure long hours of exposure. Lower down, barnacles and mussels dominate where submersion is more frequent. Lowest of all, just above the sea, anemones and kelp cling to surfaces that are almost never fully dry.

Barnacles solve the drying problem with a kind of biological vault. When the tide retreats, the animal seals itself inside its calcium plates, trapping a small reserve of seawater so its gills stay moist. Mussels take a different approach, clamping their shells shut and slowing their metabolism almost to a standstill until the water returns.

Understanding these adaptations matters beyond the classroom. The intertidal zone is a sensitive early-warning system for ocean change, and the same traits that let these animals survive the tides also shape how they will respond to warming seas and shifting acidity.
