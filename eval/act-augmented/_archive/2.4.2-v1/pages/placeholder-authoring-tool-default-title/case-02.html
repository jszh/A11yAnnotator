How to Build a Hot Compost Pile That Finishes in Three Weeks

By Dana Okafor · Updated March 14, 2026 · 8 min read

Most home compost heaps sit cold and slow for the better part of a year. A properly built hot pile, by contrast, can reach 60 °C within 48 hours and turn kitchen scraps and autumn leaves into finished compost in as little as three weeks. The trick is getting the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, moisture, and pile volume right from the start.

Get the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio right

Microbes need roughly 25 to 30 parts carbon for every part nitrogen. "Browns" — dry leaves, straw, shredded cardboard — supply carbon; "greens" — vegetable peelings, fresh grass clippings, coffee grounds — supply nitrogen. A reliable starting recipe is two to three buckets of browns for every bucket of greens.

If your pile smells like ammonia, it has too much nitrogen; add browns. If it just sits there doing nothing, it likely needs more greens and water.

Build it all at once, and big enough

Hot composting depends on critical mass. A pile smaller than one cubic metre loses heat too quickly to the surrounding air. Assemble the whole pile in a single session rather than adding scraps a handful at a time, layering browns and greens and watering each layer until it feels like a wrung-out sponge.

Turn on a schedule

Insert a compost thermometer into the centre. When the temperature climbs past 65 °C, or after about four days, turn the pile to move the cooler outer material into the hot core. Repeat every few days. Each turn re-introduces oxygen and resets the heating cycle. After three or four turns the pile will stop re-heating — that is your signal the active phase is done.

Let the finished material cure for another week before spreading it. Properly cured hot compost is dark, crumbly, and smells of forest floor, with no recognisable scraps remaining.
