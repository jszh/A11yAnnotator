The Quiet Revolution Reshaping How Our City Moves

By Dana Okafor · March 18, 2026 · 7 min read

For three decades, Meridian’s downtown grid was optimized for one thing: moving cars through it as fast as possible. Sidewalks narrowed, crossings lengthened, and the neighborhoods on either side of the arterial roads slowly emptied of foot traffic. Then, almost without anyone noticing, the city changed course.

The shift began with a single corridor on Birch Street, where planners replaced two traffic lanes with protected bike paths, widened curbs, and a row of street trees. Business owners braced for disaster. Instead, ground-floor vacancies fell by a third within a year, and pedestrian counts nearly doubled.

That philosophy — treating streets as places rather than conduits — has now spread to a dozen corridors. Transit ridership is up, collisions are down, and a generation of residents is rediscovering the simple act of walking to dinner.

Critics warn that the changes have slowed delivery traffic and frustrated commuters from the outer suburbs. City Hall counters that the trade-offs were deliberate, and that the data, so far, vindicates the gamble.
