The long tide

A salt marsh that has held the same line for two centuries is now walking inland a metre a year — and the village behind it is deciding whether to follow.

By Marisol Tan · Photographs by the author · 9 min read

Stand on the sea wall at Fenwick Hythe at the top of a spring tide and you can watch the marsh breathe. The water slides up the creeks, fills the pans, and for twenty minutes the whole green plain shivers like the skin of an animal. For two hundred years the seaward edge of that plain sat almost exactly where the old enclosure map of 1820 says it should.

What changed, the parish engineer told me as we walked the wall, is not the sea so much as the supply of sediment, because the river that once fed the marsh

“We are not losing the marsh. The marsh is leaving, and asking us to move with it.”

— Dr. Helen Okafor, coastal geomorphologist

was dammed upstream in the 1970s, so the grains that used to settle out and raise the marsh surface each winter simply never arrive, and the plain can no longer keep its head above the rising water.

The result is a marsh that is, in the engineer’s flat phrase, “migrating” — retreating landward as its seaward edge drowns. Where it meets the old sea wall it has nowhere to go, so it thins and dies, and the wall is left holding back open water it was never built to face.

Fenwick Hythe now has a choice the 1820 surveyors never imagined: hold the line with ever higher concrete, or breach the wall on purpose and let the marsh climb into the low fields behind, taking three farms and a lane with it. The parish votes in October.
