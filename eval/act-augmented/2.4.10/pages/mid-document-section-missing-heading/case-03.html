How to re-seat a leaking compression fitting under a sink

Difficulty: beginner · Time: 30–45 min · Last reviewed 2026-04-09

Overview

A weeping compression fitting on a supply line is almost always a seating problem, not a worn part. In most cases you can stop the drip by shutting the water, loosening the nut, re-seating the ferrule, and re-tightening it correctly. This guide walks through it in five steps. You should not need to replace the valve or call a plumber.

  • Adjustable wrench (two, ideally)
  • Old towel and a small bowl
  • Replacement ferrule and nut (in case the old one is scored)
  • Plumber's silicone grease (optional)

Procedure

Step 1 — Shut off the supply and drain the line

Turn the fixture shut-off valve clockwise until it stops. If there is no local shut-off, close the main. Open the faucet above to relieve pressure and let the line drain into your bowl. Put the towel under the fitting; there will be a little water left in the riser even after the line "stops".

Step 2 — Loosen and slide back the compression nut

Hold the valve body steady with one wrench so you don't twist the whole valve, and back the compression nut off with the other. Slide the nut up the tube. You will now see the brass ferrule (the little ring) seated where the tube enters the valve. Wipe everything dry so you can see what you are doing.

Step 3 — Inspect and re-seat the ferrule

Look closely at the ferrule. If it is round, smooth and unscored, you can re-use it — just clean it. If it shows a groove, a flat spot, or green crust, cut it off and slip a new ferrule on. A thin smear of silicone grease on the ferrule helps it seat evenly. Push the tube fully home into the valve so the ferrule sits squarely against the seat.

Step 4 — Re-tighten the nut to the correct point

Slide the nut back down and start it by hand so you don't cross-thread it. Then, holding the valve body with the second wrench, tighten the nut: snug it until it meets resistance, then turn it about another half to three-quarters of a turn — no more. Compression fittings seal by deforming the ferrule slightly, not by brute force; over-tightening is the single most common way people cause a leak. If you have a torque figure from the valve maker, use it instead of feel.

If the nut spins freely and never firms up, the threads are stripped — stop and replace the nut rather than forcing it.

Step 5 — Restore water and test under pressure

Slowly reopen the shut-off and watch the fitting for thirty seconds, then wipe it dry and watch again for a minute. A fitting that is dry under full pressure is sealed. If you see even a bead form, shut off, give the nut another eighth of a turn, and retest. Two small adjustments are normal; if it still weeps after that, replace the ferrule.

If it still leaks

Persistent weeping after a fresh ferrule and correct torque usually means a scored valve seat. At that point the valve itself needs replacing, which is a different (still beginner-friendly) repair covered in our shut-off-valve guide.
