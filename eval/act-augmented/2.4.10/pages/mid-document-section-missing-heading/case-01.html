Grassland Ecology Letters

Soil organic carbon recovery in the four years after livestock exclusion from a semi-arid bunchgrass prairie

A. Reyes-Olmos, D. Kavanagh & P. N. Owusu · Received 14 Jan · Accepted 2 May · Vol 28(3), pp. 211–224
https://doi.org/10.0000/gel.2026.0311

Abstract

Removing grazing pressure is widely promoted as a route to rebuilding soil carbon, yet field evidence on how quickly the topsoil pool responds is sparse for semi-arid systems. We tracked soil organic carbon (SOC) on paired grazed and exclosure plots for four growing seasons. Surface SOC rose by 11 % in the exclosures relative to grazed controls, with the gain concentrated in the upper five centimetres and driven mainly by greater fine-root turnover rather than by litter accumulation. Recovery was non-linear, stalling in the drought year, which cautions against extrapolating early gains.

Introduction

Grasslands store roughly a third of the world's terrestrial carbon, most of it below ground, and the question of whether reducing grazing pressure can rebuild that store has moved from rangeland journals into climate-policy debates. The intuition is straightforward: fewer animals means more standing biomass, more litter, deeper roots, and therefore more carbon entering the soil. Field tests of that intuition, however, disagree. Some long-running exclosures show steady carbon accrual; others show no change for a decade; a few even show losses where ungrazed swards become moribund and root turnover slows.

Most of this disagreement comes from temperate, relatively wet sites. Semi-arid bunchgrass prairies — which cover vast areas of the interior West and are under active pressure to be enrolled in carbon programmes — are far less studied, and their water-limited productivity may make them respond differently. We set out to measure the short-term trajectory of topsoil carbon after grazing is removed, and to attribute any change to its most likely mechanisms.

The study was carried out on the Cold Creek Research Range, a semi-arid bunchgrass prairie at 1,340 m elevation receiving a long-term mean of 318 mm of precipitation, two-thirds of it as winter snow.

Within the range we identified eight blocks that had been continuously grazed by cattle at a moderate stocking rate for at least three decades. In the spring of the first study year we fenced one randomly chosen half of each block to exclude livestock, leaving the paired half open to the existing grazing regime, giving eight grazed–exclosure pairs distributed across the range's two dominant soil series. The design is therefore a randomised paired-plot experiment with the block as the unit of replication, which controls for the considerable spatial heterogeneity in baseline carbon that earlier surveys of the range had reported.

Each autumn for four years we collected soil to a depth of thirty centimetres at six randomly located points per plot using a 4.8 cm corer, splitting every core into 0–5, 5–15 and 15–30 cm depth increments. Cores were composited by increment within a plot, air-dried, sieved to 2 mm, and visible roots and stones were removed by hand and by flotation. Bulk density was determined from separate intact cores so that carbon concentrations could be expressed on an equivalent-soil-mass basis, which avoids the well-known artefact of comparing fixed depths when grazing has changed how compacted the surface is.

Total carbon was measured by dry combustion on a CN elemental analyser; because the soils are non-calcareous, total carbon was taken to equal organic carbon, a simplification we checked on a subset by acid fumigation and found to introduce less than two per cent error. Fine-root biomass and annual root turnover were estimated from sequential cores and from minirhizotron tubes installed at two points per plot and imaged monthly through each growing season. Above-ground litter was clipped from fixed quadrats at peak standing crop.

Differences between treatments were tested with linear mixed-effects models that treated block as a random effect and year, treatment and their interaction as fixed effects, with depth increment fitted as a separate stratum. Carbon stocks were the response on an equivalent-mass basis; we report the treatment-by-year interaction as the test of whether exclosures diverged from grazed controls over time, and we used restricted maximum likelihood with Kenward–Roger degrees of freedom for the small number of blocks.

Results

Surface (0–5 cm) carbon stocks in the exclosures exceeded those of the paired grazed plots by an average of 11 % by the fourth year, a divergence captured by a significant treatment-by-year interaction. The two deeper increments showed no detectable treatment effect over the study period.

The accrual tracked fine-root turnover rather than litter: root turnover in the exclosures was roughly a quarter higher, while clipped litter mass did not differ between treatments. In the third (drought) year the surface gain plateaued and partially reversed before resuming.

Discussion

Four years of grazing exclusion produced a real but shallow and modest carbon gain, confined to the top five centimetres and driven by roots rather than litter. The practical implication is sobering for carbon programmes: the recoverable pool in these water-limited soils is small, slow, and vulnerable to interruption by drought, so crediting schemes that assume linear early accrual will overstate the climate benefit of de-stocking. Longer records and deeper sampling are needed before exclosure can be sold as reliable carbon storage in semi-arid prairie.
