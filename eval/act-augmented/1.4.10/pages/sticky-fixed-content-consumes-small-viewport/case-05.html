The Logic of a Hypothesis Test

A hypothesis test is, at heart, a structured way of asking a single question: is the pattern I see in my sample strong enough that I should doubt it arose by chance alone? Everything else — the null hypothesis, the test statistic, the p-value — is machinery built to answer that one question honestly.

The null hypothesis

We begin by stating a null hypothesis, written H₀, that represents "nothing interesting is happening": the new drug has no effect, the coin is fair, the two groups have the same mean. We then ask how surprising our data would be if H₀ were true. Crucially, we never set out to prove H₀; we only ever gather evidence against it.

The test statistic and the p-value

From the sample we compute a test statistic — a single number that summarises how far our data fall from what H₀ predicts. We then find the probability of seeing a statistic at least that extreme, assuming H₀ is true. That probability is the p-value.

p = P( observe a statistic this extreme | H₀ is true )

A small p-value means our data would be surprising under H₀, which gives us grounds to reject it. A large p-value means our data are entirely consistent with H₀, so we have no reason to abandon it. Note the asymmetry: we either reject H₀ or fail to reject it. We never "accept" it.

Common misreading. A p-value is not the probability that H₀ is true. It is the probability of the data given H₀, which is a different conditional entirely. Confusing the two is the single most frequent error in applied statistics.

Significance level

Before looking at the data, we choose a threshold α — commonly 0.05 — below which we will treat the result as statistically significant. Fixing α in advance protects us from the temptation to move the goalposts once we have seen which way the result points.

In the lecture that follows, we will work a full two-sample test from start to finish, and you will see every piece of this machinery turning together on real data.
