The Medieval Period of Music

The thousand years of medieval music, stretching roughly from the fall of Rome to the dawn of the Renaissance, are the foundation on which all later Western music is built. It is the era in which music was first written down, in which harmony was first reasoned about, and in which the single melodic line of plainchant slowly, deliberately, grew a second voice.

The earliest layer is the body of liturgical chant later attributed to Pope Gregory I — the repertory we now call Gregorian chant. To learn how this monophonic music was notated, and why its neumes still puzzle scholars, see our companion essay on the notation of plainchant and the rise of the staff. It is the single most consequential technical development of the era, and it is exactly the kind of detail a reader will want to follow up.

By the ninth century, scribes at monasteries such as Saint Gall were adding a second melodic line a fixed interval below the chant — the practice known as organum. What began as parallel motion at the fourth or fifth eventually loosened into freer counterpoint, and by the era of Léonin and Pérotin at Notre-Dame de Paris, three and four independent voices could sound together over a held plainchant tenor.

The later medieval period, the Ars Nova of the fourteenth century, brought rhythmic notation flexible enough to capture syncopation and complex metre. Composers such as Guillaume de Machaut wrote both sacred Masses and secular songs of startling sophistication, and the boundary between liturgical and courtly music began, for the first time, to blur.

What unites the whole millennium is a patient, cumulative experimentation: each generation adding one new capability — notation, then harmony, then rhythm, then independence of voices — to a tradition that never threw away what came before.
